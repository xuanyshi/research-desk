Impact of initial severity and progression pattern of new-onset diabetes on pancreatic cancer risk: a 15-year longitudinal nationwide cohort study

Minyoung Lee, Min Heui Yu, Wonjeong Chae, Yong-ho Lee et al.

Gut (2026), Online FirstCAS 1 TopJCR Q1IF 24.6
CancerDiabetes

Source / DOI ↗ Scholar ↗ New-onset diabetesPancreatic cancerTreatment intensityRisk stratificationReverse causationNationwide cohort

30-second read

Greater initial treatment intensity and rapid escalation marked higher relative risk, but 3-year incidence reached only 0.47% at most. The findings support enrichment, not routine imaging on this signal alone.

Question
Can initial severity and the 6-month treatment trajectory after new-onset diabetes identify people at higher pancreatic-cancer risk?
Design
A Korean NHIS-NSC longitudinal cohort included 402,663 people in multivariable analyses and 90,331 per group after full-covariate propensity matching, with follow-up of up to 15 years.
Evidence strength Moderate; risk stratification only
What was assessed
Treatment intensity at the first E11 diagnosis, change around month 6, and subsequently recorded pancreatic cancer.
Downgrade triggers
Baseline categories were no medication, oral medication, and insulin; movement to a higher category by month 6 triggered rapid progression. Cancer required C25 and V193 codes.
Impact on interpretation
Greater initial intensity or rapid escalation enters a higher-risk tier. Because treatment intensity is a proxy and reverse causation is prominent, this is a risk marker rather than evidence that diabetes deterioration causes cancer.

From new-onset diabetes records to pancreatic-cancer risk tiers

Full deep read

Greater treatment intensity at new-onset diabetes and escalation within 6 months marked higher subsequent pancreatic-cancer risk. The trajectory is a risk marker; it does not show that glucose-lowering treatment or worsening diabetes causes pancreatic cancer.

Author team

Minyoung Lee, Min Heui Yu, Wonjeong Chae, and colleagues are based at Yonsei University College of Medicine across endocrinology, preventive medicine, and gastroenterology. The paper was published online in Gut on 24 July 2026 and uses the Korean National Health Insurance Service National Sample Cohort.

The question

New-onset diabetes is already associated with pancreatic cancer, but a single yes-or-no category combines clinically different presentations. The authors asked whether more intensive treatment at diagnosis marks greater risk and whether escalation during the first 6 months adds information beyond the initial state.

The target is 【clinical risk stratification】, not the causal effect of diabetes on cancer. Pancreatic cancer can disturb glucose regulation months or years before diagnosis, so a short-lag association may reflect occult cancer causing new-onset or rapidly worsening diabetes.

What they did

The study used the Korean NHIS-NSC from 2002 to 2019. It identified adults aged at least 40 years with newly coded type 2 diabetes during 2005-2019 and no previous pancreatic cancer, then matched each person with new-onset diabetes to as many as four controls without diabetes by age, sex, and index date. The multivariable cohort included 402,663 people, 116,064 with new-onset diabetes. A second 1:1 propensity-score cohort included 90,331 people in each group.

For 【initial severity】, the computational object was treatment status at the first E11 diagnosis. The trigger was assignment to no medication, oral medication, or insulin, with higher intensity treated as greater severity. This created a three-level exposure for risk comparison. It did not directly measure HbA1c, glucose, or symptoms, and treatment choice may reflect practice as well as disease.

For 【early progression】, the computational object was the change between baseline and treatment status around month 6. Escalation to a higher category triggered the progressive group; stable or lower intensity formed the comparator. Cancer follow-up still began at the first E11 code. The classification therefore uses post-index information and is best read as a prognostic trajectory, not as proof that treatment escalation preceded tumor initiation.

Pancreatic cancer required an ICD-10 C25 diagnosis plus the V193 cancer reimbursement code. Models adjusted for demographic factors, socioeconomic position, smoking, obesity, Charlson score, multiple diseases, and surgical history. The authors also used full-covariate propensity-score matching and a sensitivity analysis among participants with health-check data.

Main findings

New-onset diabetes was associated with pancreatic cancer relative to no diabetes: the adjusted HR was 3.47 in the multivariable model and 2.66 in the fully matched model.

By initial treatment intensity, multivariable adjusted HRs versus controls were 3.01, 4.32, and 5.60 for no medication, oral medication, and insulin. The matched estimates were 2.35, 2.96, and 5.95. The gradient was stronger for cancers diagnosed within 3 years, with P<0.001 for the time interaction.

Three-year pancreatic-cancer incidence among people with new-onset diabetes was 0.19%: 0.15%, 0.23%, and 0.47% in the untreated, oral-medication, and insulin groups. Among cancers diagnosed within 36 months, the Kaplan-Meier median interval from diabetes to cancer was 8.8 months, and 67.8% had an interval longer than 4 months.

Within the same baseline category, escalation by month 6 marked higher risk than stable or reduced treatment. For example, people untreated at baseline who then initiated treatment had an adjusted HR of 6.50, compared with 3.67 among baseline oral-medication users without escalation; matched estimates were 4.19 and 2.53. These are comparisons between trajectory groups and do not show that starting medication raises cancer risk.

How strong is the evidence?

The nationwide sample, follow-up of up to 15 years, two matching strategies, and health-check sensitivity analysis make the gradient internally consistent. Separating baseline state from short-term change is also more clinically informative than a binary new-onset-diabetes indicator.

The boundaries are substantial. Diabetes, smoking, and obesity were largely defined from claims; cancer stage, HbA1c, postprandial glucose, and actual medication adherence were unavailable. Treatment intensity is an imperfect proxy for severity and is influenced by clinicians, coverage, and contraindications. Propensity matching cannot remove residual confounding, and transportability beyond the Korean health system remains uncertain.

Reverse causation is the central problem. The association was strongest within 3 years and the median interval was only 8.8 months, a pattern compatible with occult pancreatic cancer causing glycaemic deterioration. The 6-month trajectory also uses information after the index date while counting events from that date. Future work should prespecify a landmark, separate early cancers, and combine glycaemic measures, weight loss, and pancreatic-cancer biomarkers in dynamic prediction.

Large relative risks do not establish a screening benefit. Three-year absolute incidence in every subgroup remained below the 1%-2% threshold cited for routine imaging. New-onset diabetes or treatment escalation alone therefore does not justify population-wide MRI or endoscopic ultrasonography; further enrichment and prospective validation are required.

My assessment

The useful increment is that 【treatment trajectory adds information beyond a one-time category】. Initial intensity and 6-month escalation could contribute to a dynamic model alongside age, weight change, glucose trajectory, pancreatitis history, and biomarkers.

The current evidence supports identifying people for further assessment. It does not support treating escalation as a carcinogenic exposure or using it alone to mandate imaging. Clinical utility requires a prospective landmark cohort or nested screening study reporting detection, stage shift, false positives, complications, and cost.

Relevance to my research

This paper maps directly to the 【new-onset diabetes and pancreatic cancer】 relation in an endocrine disease and cancer graph. The relation should be split into at least three time-specific propositions: whether long-standing metabolic diabetes causes pancreatic cancer, whether occult pancreatic cancer produces new-onset diabetes, and whether rapid deterioration after onset predicts near-term cancer diagnosis.

Triangulation should stratify 0-6 months, 6-36 months, and more than 3 years after diabetes onset, while retaining the diabetes definition, treatment intensity, glycaemic measures, and weight change. Strong attenuation after excluding early cancers would favor reverse causation. A causal contribution of diabetes becomes more plausible only when longer-lag epidemiology converges with genetic, mechanistic, and quasi-experimental evidence.
