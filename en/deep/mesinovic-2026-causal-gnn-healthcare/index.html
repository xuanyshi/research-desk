Five-step audit of a causal GNN claim

Full deep read

This Perspective classifies causal graph neural networks for healthcare into three levels: causally inspired, causally structured, and causally validated. It is useful as a framework for naming methods and auditing evidence. It is not a systematic review, and it does not establish that causal GNNs generally outperform associational models or are ready to support clinical decisions.

The authors

Munib Mesinovic and Tingting Zhu are based in the Department of Engineering Science at the University of Oxford. Max Buhlan is affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine at Leipzig University and the Oxford Centre for Clinical Magnetic Resonance Research. The Perspective was received on 12 November 2025, accepted on 12 June 2026, and published online in Nature Biomedical Engineering on 7 August 2026.

The problem

Healthcare AI can perform well in a single-centre test and deteriorate when hospitals, scanners, workflows, or patient populations change. Predictive models may also learn proxies such as costs, prescribing habits, or measurement protocols, mistaking institutional patterns for disease mechanisms.

The authors examine whether combining causal inference with GNNs can address these failures and organize existing work under a common audit framework. The paper covers structural causal models, causal representations, interventional prediction, counterfactual generation, robustness across environments, causal fairness, and patient-specific digital twins.

How the framework is organized

The Perspective begins with three objects from Pearl's causal hierarchy. Level 1 estimates P(Y|X), an associational quantity under observation. Level 2 estimates P(Y|do(X)), the outcome under an intervention. Level 3 computes a unit-level counterfactual under an intervention that did not occur. A model trained only on P(Y|X) cannot derive the latter two quantities without additional causal assumptions, regardless of predictive accuracy.

GNNs learn graph representations through neighbourhood message passing. Their form resembles causal graphs, but message passing can represent propagation of causal influence only when edges are causal and the structural equations and identification conditions are valid. Protein interactions, functional connectivity, comorbidity networks, and knowledge graphs carry different evidentiary meanings; graph structure alone does not place them on the same causal footing.

The authors organize methods by five capabilities: separating causal from shortcut features, estimating interventions, generating individual counterfactuals, maintaining stability across environments, and auditing fairness by causal pathway. Table 1 states triggering assumptions. Causal discovery, for example, depends on faithfulness and must specify whether latent confounding is allowed. Path-specific fairness requires a correctly specified graph among sensitive attributes, mediators, and outcomes, with relevant confounders measured.

Three evidence tiers

Tier 1 covers causally inspired architectures. They use invariance, debiasing, or causal ideas but make no explicit causal claim about learned relations. Evaluation can remain focused on prediction, cross-environment stability, and explanatory consistency.

Tier 2 covers causally structured methods that learn a causal structure or estimate an effect. Structural assumptions, causal sufficiency, identification, and positivity must be defensible in the application, with consistency against external knowledge or established mechanisms. Predictive AUC alone does not trigger a causal conclusion.

Tier 3 covers claims of newly discovered causal relations. The proposed standard requires biological plausibility, replication in at least two independent cohorts, experimental or prospective interventional validation, and sensitivity analysis for strong claims, including E-values. Missing components should lower the tier or narrow the claim.

Box 3 gives an illustrative amyloid and cognitive-decline calculation. Biological plausibility, replication, experiments, prospective validation, and sensitivity analysis receive weights of 0.20, 0.25, 0.30, 0.15, and 0.10. With the first two supporting, the next two opposing, and sensitivity neutral, the aggregate score is zero. The example shows how interventional evidence can defeat an observational association. The weights are not externally calibrated and should not be reused as a general scoring instrument.

Applications synthesized in the paper

In brain networks, CI-GNN and DisC attempt to separate disease-related subgraphs from scanner environments or global physiological noise. CI-GNN was assessed with leave-one-site-out validation across 17 ABIDE sites and exceeded a comparator at most sites. The recovered regional connections still need multimodal neuroimaging or electrophysiological validation.

For cancer, CautionGCN selects candidate causal genes for subtype classification from differentially expressed genes, relying on causal sufficiency and faithfulness. MOGONET integrates multiple omics views but primarily models cross-omics correlations and classification, not formal causal identification. PDGrapher approximates a causal graph with protein-interaction networks and predicts combinatorial perturbation targets across nine cancer cell lines. Protein-interaction edges still mix direct mechanisms and indirect associations.

Other applications include psychiatric subtyping, seizure prediction, postoperative complications, medication recommendation, blood-pressure monitoring, cognitive decline, and fairness decomposition. Tables 2 and 3 distinguish demonstrated uses from potential translation. Real-time monitoring, cross-institutional deployment, and complete patient-specific causal digital twins largely remain research proposals.

Negative evidence and failure conditions

The Perspective does not assume that causal methods have an automatic advantage. It cites a study of 16 prediction tasks across healthcare, employment, education, and social policy. Models using all features outperformed causal-feature-only predictors both in and out of domain, while causal domain-generalization methods did not beat standard models. Only sufficiently large shifts may make robustness gains from discarding non-causal features outweigh the lost predictive information.

The initial CausalBench evaluation on large-scale single-cell perturbation data similarly found that established interventional methods did not outperform observational-only methods. Later challenge methods used interventions more effectively but had to relax common assumptions such as acyclicity and causal sufficiency. Small samples, high-dimensional neuroimaging graphs, and model variance remain problems. In EHRs, unmeasured clinical judgement, patient preferences, and selection mechanisms can leave apparently coherent structures driven by residual confounding.

Box 2 reports one computational reference point: CI-GNN identifies causal subgraphs on 116-node brain networks in 32.8 ms per instance at inference. This supports latency feasibility for moderately sized graphs. It does not cover training or graph construction and cannot be extrapolated to causal discovery among roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes.

How solid is the evidence

The evidence is limited for clinical efficacy. This is an expert Perspective synthesizing 151 references with method tables, assumptions, and a translational framework. It reports no systematic search, inclusion process, risk-of-bias assessment, or effect-size synthesis. The reviewed studies span synthetic data, single-centre retrospective cohorts, multicentre datasets, cell perturbations, and non-healthcare benchmarks, so their performance metrics are not directly comparable.

The 94% to 73% performance change and 87% to 95% survival values in Figure 1, along with the curves in Figure 2, are conceptual illustrations. Box 4 is explicitly an imagined clinical workflow. Its 12%, 18%, and 22% cardiovascular risk reductions and eGFR changes for three antihypertensive options are not trial results or validated model outputs. They explain a workflow and should not guide prescribing.

The most defensible conclusion is an audit boundary. Prediction, graph explanations, and stable features do not automatically establish causal identification. Node semantics and biological resolution determine which interventions are meaningful. Causal claims require explicit estimands, structural assumptions, validation designs, and failure conditions.
