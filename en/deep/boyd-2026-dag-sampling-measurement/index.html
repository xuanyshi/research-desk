Why your DAG should probably incorporate sampling and measurement processes

Robin J Boyd, Rob Cooke, Gary D Powney, Oliver L Pescott

Current Opinion in Insect Science 78 (2026):101578

When triggered, a DAG should add sample inclusion S, true variables, measured proxies, and common causes of measurement. These nodes expose forced conditioning, selection bias, and shared measurement error that can defeat identification.

When a DAG only represents the natural system, how do sampling and measurement alter identification of descriptive, predictive, and causal estimands?

Full deep read

A DAG limited to causal relations in the target system can omit the assumptions that determine whether an estimand is identified from data. The authors argue for adding sampling, missingness, and measured proxies so that descriptive, predictive, and causal estimands share an explicit observation-process audit.

The authors

Robin J Boyd, Rob Cooke, Gary D Powney, and Oliver L Pescott are based at the UK Centre for Ecology and Hydrology and the University of Exeter. This open-access review in Current Opinion in Insect Science was available online on 17 July 2026 under a CC BY license.

The problem

DAGs in ecology and insect science mainly represent causal relations among variables that exist before data collection, often with the task reduced to finding a confounder adjustment set. Every observed association also passes through sampling and measurement: which units enter the data and how true variables become observed proxies can determine identification. A graph may state assumptions about nature while leaving implicit why the data represent the target population.

The authors extend the point beyond causal effects. Descriptive and predictive questions also require assumptions about sample coverage, outcome measurement, and whether missingness can be ignored.

The approach

The paper uses Purple Emperor butterfly occupancy in the United Kingdom throughout. Its descriptive estimand is the proportion of one-kilometre squares occupied; its predictive estimand is occupancy probability conditional on woodland cover and temperature; and its causal estimand is the average change under an intervention on woodland cover. Each question first fixes the unit-level quantity and target population before asking whether available data identify it.

For the sampling criterion, the objects are the true outcome Y in the target population, sample inclusion S, and variables X that may govern inclusion. Sampling is ignorable when no open path connects S and Y. It is conditionally ignorable when measured non-colliders block that path. If S depends directly on Y, or forced conditioning on S opens a collider path between exposure and outcome, the estimand is not identified from the current data alone.

For the measurement criterion, the objects are true exposure X, true outcome Y, measured proxies Xs and Ys, and factors M affecting measurement. When the two proxies share a cause such as observer expertise, their association contains an additional non-causal path. The trigger means that the true causal effect requires a measurement model, validation data, or stronger assumptions; conventional covariate adjustment is insufficient.

Key results

Box 1 gives an identification region. If only 10% of UK squares are sampled and occupancy in the sample is 0.60, while occupancy outside the sample is completely unknown, national occupancy can only be bounded from 0.06 to 0.96. Adding independence between inclusion and occupancy point-identifies the value at 0.60. A stronger assumption excludes more possible values but increases the risk of excluding the truth.

Figure 1 compares four causal scenarios. Even after temperature confounding is measured and adjusted, sample inclusion can be a collider linking exposure and outcome. Because the analyst can only study S = 1, forced conditioning opens that path and leaves the target effect unidentified. Shared observer expertise as a cause of both exposure and outcome proxies adds another unidentified path.

Figure 2 separates ignorable, conditionally ignorable, and non-ignorable sampling. The first two require Y and S to be unconditionally independent or conditionally independent given X. In the third, S is directly affected by Y, so descriptive means and conditional predictions are not identified from the sample.

How solid is the evidence

Evidence level: limited. This is a conceptual review with no new dataset, simulation comparison, or correction experiment. Its numerical range follows directly from the law of total probability, and the graph examples align with established selection-bias, missing-data, and measurement-error theory. They test identification logic but do not show that adding nodes automatically improves estimation or reproducibility.

Table 1 lists nine insect-science applications known to the authors and is not a systematic search. The paper shows which assumptions should be explicit but does not provide a complete identification algorithm. Complex survey weights, longitudinal dropout, dependent misclassification, validation substudies, and probability sampling are outside its worked examples.
