SPIRIT-CONSORT-ELM: element-level annotated dataset and large language model approach for assessing randomized controlled trial reporting

Lan Jiang, Xiangji Ying, Andrew W. Brown, Mengfei Lan et al.

npj Digital Medicine (2026), Article in Press

Source / DOI ↗ Scholar ↗ Large language modelsInformation extractionBenchmarkingRandomized controlled trialsReporting guidelines

30-second read

Automatic-pipeline F1 is 0.822 versus 0.893 with expert snippets. Retrieval is a major improvement target; completeness does not establish low risk of bias.

Question
Can element-level SPIRIT and CONSORT questions reliably identify missing information in trial protocols and results publications?
Design
Element-level annotation of 200 paired articles and internal testing; PubMedBERT retrieves evidence before an LLM answers 119 structured questions.
Evidence strength Moderate; internal reporting assessment only
What was assessed
Agreement between model answers and reference labels for 119 questions in 20 held-out protocol-results pairs, averaging across questions after within-question decomposition.
Downgrade triggers
Missing snippets or unmet conditions trigger automatic labels, while incorrect preceding answers propagate to conditional questions. Audit errors separately for human-answered and rule-assigned instances; no deployment acceptance threshold is established.
Impact on interpretation
Can flag reporting details for expert review. It does not establish trial-conduct quality, low risk of bias or high certainty of evidence.

Full deep read

Element-level questions locate specific omissions in RCT reports; performance remains constrained by evidence retrieval and the construction of reference labels.

The authors

Lan Jiang and Xiangji Ying are joint first authors. Halil Kilicoglu at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign School of Information Sciences is the corresponding author, collaborating with epidemiology researchers at UNC and other institutions. The study extends the team's SPIRIT-CONSORT-TM corpus. The article was published on 6 October 2026 as an Article in Press.

The problem

A reporting item can contain several elements, such as dose, frequency and treatment duration. Detecting any item-related text can conceal partial reporting. This study turns the assessment into structured questions about what trial protocols and results publications actually report.

The approach

Experts derive 119 questions from 83 fine-grained items based on SPIRIT 2013 and CONSORT 2010. There are 85 check-one and 34 check-all-that-apply questions; 26 questions are conditional. Multi-element questions are included within these categories.

The corpus comprises 100 protocol-results pairs, or 200 articles. Fifty articles undergo independent double annotation and reconciliation; one annotator assesses the remaining 150. Of 19,500 applicable question instances, 9,892 receive explicit human responses and 9,608 are assigned automatically because relevant snippets are absent or triggering conditions are unmet.

The label rules preserve distinct states. Missing universally applicable elements receive No or Not reported; a preceding answer establishing inapplicability triggers Not applicable; unresolved applicability triggers No text to assess. These states should not be collapsed into a single missing-information label.

A fine-tuned PubMedBERT model retrieves item-related sentences, followed by structured question answering with GPT-5 or Qwen-2.5. Evaluation retains 20 held-out protocol-results pairs, with the other 80 pairs available for training, prompt development and example selection. Multiple-answer questions are split into binary sub-items to calculate within-question micro-F1; final scores average across 119 questions. Confidence intervals use 10,000 bootstrap iterations.

Key results

Mean inter-annotator Gwet’s AC1 is 0.782. Automatic retrieval plus GPT-5 achieves F1 = 0.822 (95% CI 0.794–0.847) and AC1 = 0.796. Expert snippets raise F1 to 0.893 (0.872–0.913). The best single-example strategy reaches only 0.830.

The second-assessor experiment includes just six articles. Model agreement with reconciled labels is AC1 = 0.684 with automatic retrieval and 0.763 with expert snippets, below human inter-annotator agreement of 0.841 on that subset. Direct full-text prompting yields F1 = 0.476 in an experiment limited to five article pairs and ten questions; that estimate cannot characterize all full-text approaches.

How solid is the evidence

Splitting by protocol-results pair reduces the risk of placing the same trial in different partitions. However, the 40 test articles come from PMC open-access trials with paired protocols and results. There is no external validation. Reference labels rely on previously annotated snippets, so incomplete evidence coverage can affect both annotation and model evaluation.

F1 measures agreement with reference answers about reporting elements. It does not validate trial conduct, risk of bias or causal certainty. Roughly half the corpus labels are generated by rules; performance should also be reported separately for directly answered and automatically assigned instances. Conditional questions propagate upstream errors. Alignment with the 2025 reporting guidelines remains incomplete.
